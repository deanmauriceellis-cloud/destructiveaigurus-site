WHAT WE ACTUALLY DO

Salem is a dataset.

We build engines that absorb data and turn it into something you can walk through, fly over, or ask a question of. Salem is simply the dataset we are allowed to show you in public. Yours would go in the same slot — and it would never leave your building.

ENGINE 01

A phone that knows where it is and what is around it.

It works with no signal, no account and no connection, because everything it needs is already on the device.

WHAT IT ABSORBS

Any set of points that have a location and something to say. The underlying record has no concept of history, tourism or Salem — it is id, name, lat, lng, category, some content, a geofence radius, a priority, and an optional voice clip.

WHAT SALEM PUT IN IT

  • ~2,200 places, 21 categories
  • 1,408 photographs
  • ~2,700 recorded clips
  • 313 MB of maps, offline

WHAT ELSE FITS THE SLOT

  • every asset in a plant
  • every inspection point on a line
  • every unit in a property portfolio
  • every stop on a service route
The interesting part is not the map. It is that a technician in a basement, a surveyor in a valley, or an inspector inside a steel tank gets the same behaviour as someone standing in full signal — because nothing was ever going to be fetched.
ENGINE 02

Any number that varies across a map, turned into ground you can fly over.

A surface, a classification, and the things that stand on it. That is the whole input contract.

WHAT IT ABSORBS

One raster for the surface, one for the classification, a point file for what stands on it, and a coordinate system. In Salem the surface happened to be elevation from a public survey — 576² of it, spanning 9,216 m, ranging −3.2 … 67.9.

WHAT SALEM PUT IN IT

  • 38,054 buildings, massed from survey
  • 2,048² landcover, 19 classes
  • 1,706 historic sites
  • 27 bridges, 5 lighthouses

WHAT ELSE FITS THE SLOT

  • population — fly over demographics
  • signal strength — fly over coverage
  • revenue, claims, churn, risk
  • anything with a value per cell
The renderer does not know those numbers were metres. It reads a value per cell and builds a surface out of it. Feed it people instead of elevation and you are flying over a population. Feed it exposure and you are flying over your own risk — and you will see the shape of it in about four seconds, which is the entire argument for looking at data this way.

All of it runs on hardware you own. That is not a preference — it is the product.

Everything above was built and rendered on one workstation in Massachusetts. The maps, the artwork, the voices, the models that made them: nothing was sent out to be processed by anyone else, and there is no service in the middle that has to be trusted.

Which is precisely why you can put your actual data in it. The reason most organisations demo AI on sanitised sample data is that the real data cannot safely go where the tool lives. Move the tool to the data and that problem stops existing.

The same discipline is what turns these into defensive instruments rather than another thing to secure — a system that never needed to reach out is a system with far less to protect.
